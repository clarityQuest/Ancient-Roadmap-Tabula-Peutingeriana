Imagine a map of the entire Roman world, drawn on a single scroll nearly seven metres long and just thirty-four centimetres tall. It charts the roads of the known world, from the Atlantic coast of Britain to the Indian subcontinent. This is the Tabula Peutingeriana, one of the most remarkable documents to survive from antiquity.

Its story begins in the fourth or fifth century, with a Roman original. That original is lost. What survives is a copy made around the year twelve hundred by a monk in Colmar, in Alsace, probably from an earlier Carolingian copy.

In fourteen ninety-four, Konrad Celtes discovered the map and passed it to Konrad Peutinger of Augsburg, who gave it the name it still carries. After Peutinger's death it passed through various hands, until in seventeen thirty-eight it entered the Imperial Library in Vienna. It is kept there to this day, in what is now the Austrian National Library, and since two thousand and seven it has been part of UNESCO's Memory of the World.

But this is not a map in the modern sense. The long, narrow scroll forced its maker to squeeze the world from north to south. The Mediterranean becomes a thin strip, and Italy lies almost on its side. What mattered was not shape, but connection: the roads, the distances between stations in Roman miles, and the cities they linked. Around three and a half thousand names fill its twelve segments.

Three cities stand out above all others. Rome, Constantinople and Antioch each appear as an enthroned figure, a sign of their supreme importance in the late Roman world.

One part of the map is missing. The first segment, which showed most of Britain, the Iberian Peninsula and the Atlantic coast of Morocco, has been lost since at least the sixteenth century. The other eleven segments survive complete.
